TESSERA Clusterings · DAT300 / DIT616 · group 5

Spatial smoothing, over-cluster + merge and HDBSCAN for TEE's clustering

Can we make TEE's k-means maps less noisy by using neighbouring pixels, more stable by over-clustering and merging, and is HDBSCAN a better clusterer for cities? Tested on Gothenburg (TEE viewport, 2024) and an Austrian crop tile with 2 569 labelled fields.

Christopher Kröss · 30 Sep 2026 (over-cluster section added the same day) · code: TESSERA/experiments/spatial_hdbscan/

Bottom line

  1. Recommended pipeline: over-cluster to 32 → Ward merge → refine → light MRF smoothing. Compared with TEE today, mean seed agreement (ARI) rises from 0.63 to 0.96 on Gothenburg and from 0.62 to 0.82 on Austria. Speckle inside fields falls from 25 % to 15 %. In the browser it should add roughly 0.5 s to today's run (estimate from the measured steps in section 6).
  2. Smoothing works, it is cheap, and it fits into TEE's browser code. A Gaussian pre-filter of 10 m or a light MRF cuts speckle from 25 % to about 15 % and puts more of the remaining cluster edges on real field boundaries. A majority filter removes more speckle but also erases real edges. Smoothing does not make runs agree across seeds.
  3. Over-cluster + merge on its own does not help. Merging 32–128 centroids with Ward gives maps that fit the data slightly worse than k-means and are less stable than 10 restarts. Average linkage makes one giant cluster, and a spatial merge changes little. It only pays off as a starting point: a few k-means iterations from the merged centroids give the most stable maps of all methods tested, and they fit as well as 10 restarts.
  4. HDBSCAN is not worth pursuing for TEE. On Gothenburg it puts 93 % of all pixels into one cluster. The settings that split the city further mark about 50 to 75 % of the sample as noise and are less stable than k-means with the same number of clusters. TESSERA embeddings form a continuum without gaps in density, so a density-based method has little to find.

What was tested

Every method runs on the same embeddings, with the same metrics, over several seeds. Smoothing radii are given in metres, not pixels (see the note on TEE's grid below).

Data

Gothenburg 2024: the TEE viewport "Gteborg", 385 × 720 pixels, decoded exactly as TEE does (RVQ codebooks). No labels yet, so only unsupervised metrics and maps. 20 seeds, k = 5.

Austria, tile 16.75 E / 48.35 N: 1 128 × 766 pixels at native 10 m (EPSG:32633), from TEE's compute cache. 438 419 pixels fall in 2 569 INVEKOS fields. 10 seeds, k = 17.

Methods

Baselines: TEE's k-means as in segmentation.js, and the same with 10 restarts (best inertia).

Smoothing (on top of the restart version): majority filter on the label map; Gaussian or edge-aware bilateral filter on the embeddings before k-means; MRF / Potts smoothing solved with ICM.

Over-cluster + merge: k-means with K0 = 32, 64 or 128 centroids, merged to k with Ward, average linkage or a spatially weighted Ward; optionally refined with k-means iterations from the merged centroids.

HDBSCAN: PCA (8, 16, 32 dims) or UMAP on a 20 000-pixel sample, HDBSCAN on the sample, then every pixel takes the label of its nearest sample points.

1 · Smoothing removes speckle

Gothenburg city centre, about 1.6 × 1.6 km, seed 42, k = 5. Colours are matched between panels.

The first two panels differ only in the k-means initialisation, which shows how much of the map depends on the seed today. Every smoothing variant turns the speckled patches into coherent areas. The majority filter produces the roundest blobs and erases thin structures first.

2 · Which smoothing keeps real edges

Fewer cluster changes inside a field are good. Fewer cluster changes across a field boundary are bad. The field polygons let us measure both on the Austrian tile.

Speckle (x-axis) is the share of neighbouring pixel pairs inside one field that get different clusters. Edge ratio (y-axis) is how much more often clusters change across a field boundary than inside a field. A method that only removes noise moves left and up. A method that blurs everything moves left and down.

  • TEE today: speckle 25 %, edge ratio 1.75×.
  • Gaussian pre-filter 10 m: speckle 15 %, edge ratio 1.91×. This is the best trade-off. MRF β 0.25 is almost identical (15 %, 1.89×).
  • Majority filter 20 m: the least speckle (10 %), but the edge ratio drops to 1.57×. It also erases field boundaries, so small objects like roads and narrow fields are at risk.

3 · Stability comes from restarts, not from smoothing

If you run the same method with different seeds, how similar are the maps? An ARI of 1 means identical maps.

Smoothing only changes pixels near the decision boundaries. If one seed merges the river with the city and another seed does not, smoothing both maps leaves them just as different. The Gaussian and bilateral pre-filters can help a little on Gothenburg because they make the embedding clouds tighter, but the effect is not consistent on Austria. To make the maps stable, TEE needs restarts or a better initialisation, and section 5 shows an even better one. The smoothing step then comes on top.

Leaf selection splits Gothenburg into about 20 clusters, but most of the sample becomes noise, and k-means with the same k is more stable. On Austria, HDBSCAN is closer: at the same k its fields are more uniform than with k-means, but mostly because it makes a few very large clusters (see "Largest"). It also changes cluster less often across field boundaries and is less stable in every pairing. The embeddings of a city or a farm region are one continuous cloud, not separate dense groups. Density-based methods are designed for the second case. This also answers the "DBSCAN for cities" idea from the 24 Sep meeting.

5 · Over-cluster + merge: useful only as a starting point

k-means with many centroids (K0), then the K0 centroids are merged pairwise down to k. Ward merges the pair that increases the within-cluster variance the least. Average linkage merges by mean centroid distance. Spatial Ward also favours pairs that share a lot of boundary on the map. "Refine" runs up to 20 k-means iterations on the sample, starting from the merged group means.

ARI = mean agreement between seeds (20 seeds Gothenburg, 10 Austria). "Fit vs restarts" = within-cluster variance on all pixels relative to k-means with 10 restarts (lower is better). Speckle and edge ratio are means over the seeds.

  • Merging alone: over-cluster 32 → Ward is more stable than TEE today on Gothenburg (0.81), but not more stable than 10 restarts, and on Austria it barely changes (ARI 0.66). More centroids do not help: K0 = 128 is less stable on Austria (0.55). The merged groups also fit the data slightly worse than k-means, because Ward only sees the centroids and never moves a pixel.
  • Average linkage joins everything into one big group (60 % of the pixels in Gothenburg). It looks smooth, but only because most of the map has one colour, like HDBSCAN.
  • Spatial Ward (merge clusters that touch a lot) changes almost nothing. Merging acts on 32–64 cluster centroids, so it cannot fix pixel-level noise.
  • Merge, then refine: the merged centroids are a very good starting point for k-means. ARI reaches 0.96 on Gothenburg and 0.83 on Austria, both higher than 10 restarts, with the same fit. This works with a 10 000-pixel over-clustering sample. The Ward weights here come from all pixels; building them from the sample alone (needed for the fast browser version) should behave the same but is not tested yet. It is a known idea: hierarchical or refined starting points for k-means (Milligan 1980; Bradley & Fayyad 1998).
  • Plus smoothing: MRF β 0.25 on top keeps the stability (ARI 0.82 on Austria) and brings speckle down to 15 % with an edge ratio of 1.87×. The Gaussian pre-filter lowers stability on Austria (see table), probably because blurred pixels at field edges connect similar crop clusters. So MRF is the better partner here.

A merge tree has one more benefit: it contains the result for every k. TEE could show a k slider that changes the map without re-running k-means: relabelling the pixels from the tree takes milliseconds. This is a usability feature, not an accuracy gain.

  • Smoothing: the majority filter uses one integral image per cluster, so it is O(N · k) and takes 8–18 ms. The MRF needs the distance of each pixel to every centroid, which the worker already computes in its final assignment pass, so keeping them costs nothing extra. The Gaussian pre-filter touches all 128 channels (526 ms) but runs only once per viewport and can be cached across k and seed changes.
  • Over-cluster + merge: TEE's sample rule (500 × k pixels) makes over-clustering expensive: K0 = 32 takes 1.9 s, mostly for assigning every pixel to 32 centroids. The pipeline does not need that pass. Over-clustering only the 10 000-pixel sample takes 382 ms, the Ward merge about 3 ms, and the refine step and final assignment cost the same as today's k-means.
  • HDBSCAN: the npm package hdbscan-ts builds a full n × n JavaScript array and needs 3.6 minutes for 2 000 points, so it cannot be used. A lean implementation without the matrix needs 2.1 s for a 10 000-point sample and 8.3 s for 20 000, plus the assignment of all pixels. That would be possible in a worker, but it would bring the quality problems above to the browser.

7 · How to proceed

  1. Replace TEE's single k-means++ run with the merge-then-refine start: over-cluster to 32 on a 10 000-pixel sample, merge with Ward, then run the existing Lloyd loop from the merged centroids. It is the most stable method tested and costs about 0.4 s more in the browser. Keep the seed input so runs stay reproducible.
  2. Add smoothing as a UI option "Spatial smoothing: off / light / strong". Light = MRF β 0.25, strong = MRF β 0.5, both using the distances the worker already computes. The majority filter is a cheap fallback. Define all windows in metres from the geotransform, not in pixels.
  3. Offer a k slider from the merge tree as an optional usability feature, and mention it in the upstream PR.
  4. Coordinate with Elias: this section overlaps with his part. He can check the results, extend the sweep (other K0, number of refine iterations, sample sizes), and own the segmentation.js port of the merge step.
  5. Evaluate the class-level effect with 2022 embeddings. The Austrian labels are from 2022, but TEE's cache holds 2024 embeddings. Field geometry barely changes between years, so the field metrics above are valid. Crops rotate, though, so crop-class AMI (about 0.08 here) is not meaningful until the 2022 tiles are downloaded (about 110 MB per tile).
  6. Test small objects on Gothenburg once the hand labels (roads, buildings, river) are exported. That is where smoothing can do harm, and Austria's large fields cannot show it.
  7. Report HDBSCAN and plain merging as negative results, and keep them out of the TEE implementation.

Caveats

  • TEE's grid is not square: Gothenburg pixels are about 7 m wide and 13 m tall, because TEE resamples to latitude/longitude. 30 % of horizontal and 17 % of vertical neighbour pairs are exact duplicates, which inflates coherence scores in TEE's grid. That is why the quantitative field metrics use the native 10 m Austrian tile.
  • Different random number generator: the Python baseline uses NumPy's RNG instead of TEE's mulberry32, so ARI values differ from the 26 Sep measurement (mean 0.79 over seeds 1–20). The pattern (restarts ≫ smoothing) holds in both datasets.
  • Single seed for edge metrics: speckle and edge ratio were computed on the seed-42 maps; the differences between the best methods (1.87–1.91×) are within the expected seed noise.
  • Over-cluster runs: K0 centroids use TEE's sample rule (500 × K0) unless marked "10 000 px". Fit is measured as within-cluster variance on the raw embeddings, averaged over the first five seeds.
  • One tile only: the Austrian results come from one tile of about 866 000 pixels, which is the 10 % development subset Romaric suggested. Full-size runs belong in the final experiments.
